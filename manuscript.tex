\section{1. Introduction}
The analysis of panel data, in which the same people are repeatedly measured on the same variables, is wide-spread in psychology and related disciplines to investigate how variables causally affect each other over time. Such a causal interpretation is sometimes explicit, but is typically left implicit through the of specific language or suggestions for interventions based on analysis results \parencite{asendorpf_modeling_2021, grosz_taboo_2020, hamaker_description_2020}. To identify such causal effects from data, it is critical that the research question and the research design are aligned. In this article, we specifically focus on misalignment of the temporal characteristics of a causal process under study and the timescale of measurements, and the consequences hereof for the identification and estimation of a causal effect from observed longitudinal data \parencite{hamaker_within-between_2023, voelkle_role_2018, mitchell_building_2001, collins_analysis_2006}. For example, if a causal process is characterized by within-day or day-to-day dynamics, then measurements should be taken at approximately the same frequency for the measurements to be informative about the process of interest. If, in contrast, the temporal design of a study is misaligned with the temporal dynamics of a causal process (e.g., the measurement frequency concerns months or years), it is possible that valid causal effects cannot be estimated from data, even if we had an infinite amount of measurements \parencite{collins_effect_2002}.

This article centers on two common measurement-related features in panel data that can lead to temporal misalignment, namely \textit{time-aggregation} of variables and \textit{low process coverage} due to systematic sampling. Time-aggregation refers to measurements that pertain to an average level of a construct across a particular time-period, rather than the level of the construct at the moment of measurement \parencite{petkovic_aggregation_2009}. For example, in psychological research, it is relatively common to ask participants of panel studies to provide aggregate scores themselves, by using questions that refer to participants' average levels of anxiety or self-esteem across \textit{the past week} or \textit{the past month}, rather than their levels of anxiety or self-esteem \textit{right now}. The concept of low process coverage refers to the situation where the combination of systematic sampling over time (i.e., taking measurements at regular intervals) and time-aggregation of measurements only captures a small part of a continuous process. For example, \textcite{hamaker_within-between_2023} describes how the combination of biannual measurement occasions with measurements referring to the past week, implies that less then 1\% of an ongoing process is actually covered by measurements. Both of these features can negatively impact a researcher's ability to identify and estimate particular causal effects from data. Therefore, the current paper considers this impact in the context of causal inference using panel data, and demonstrates it using multiple simulation scenarios. 

This paper is organized as follows. To aid explanation of time-aggregation and low process coverage, we first introduce these concepts in the context of single-subject (i.e., $N = 1$) research with many repeated measures. Section 2 provides an overview of early analytical work on the impact of time-aggregation of variables on the dynamic characteristics of dynamic models for $N = 1$ data. It also introduces the impact of low process coverage from both a continuous-time modeling and causal inference perspective. In Section 3, we present a simulation study to extend previous analytical work. These simulations focus on (1) the combined influence of time-aggregation and low process coverage for $N = 1$ data; (2) time-aggregation and low process coverage in $N = 1$ data in the presence of a time-varying confounder; and (3) time aggregation and low process coverage for \textit{panel data} and in the presence of a time-varying confounder. Section 4 presents simulation results. Section 5 discusses the implications of the results for current causal research using panel data, and suggests future directions for addressing some of the concerns raised. Throughout, we use a running example that is motivated by panel research on the impact of social media use on anxiety in early-adolescents. Specifically, we take inspiration from the Longitudinal Internet studies for the Social Sciences (LISS) panel, which consists of a random sample of Dutch households representative of the Dutch-speaking population in the Netherlands, and which contains annual measurements covering a large variety of domains \parencite{das_true_2018}. In the LISS panel, anxiety was measured with the statement ``This past month... I felt very anxious.'', with participants having to indicate which answer best described them on a scale from ``1 = never'' to ``6 = continuously''. Social media usage was measured by asking participants how many hours per week \textit{on average} they spend on social media. 


\section{2. Background}
Much of the early work on the impact of time-aggregation of variables and systematic sampling comes from the econometric literature, and was driven by the time-aggregation of economic variables before analyses. For instance, the aggregation of \textit{momentary} stock prices to \textit{day-averages}, or the transformation of \textit{monthly} gross domestic product figures to \textit{quarterly} aggregates \parencite{mundlak_aggregation_1961, telser_discrete_1967}. This work focuses specifically on dynamic models for time series data, such as the lag-1 autoregressive model, the AR(1), and its extension to a time-varying covariate $X$, the ARX(1). Because these dynamic models form the backbone of many of the current statistical models used to analyze panel data in psychology \parencite[e.g., the class of cross-lagged panel models;][]{usami_unified_2019}, we introduce these dynamic models first in Section 2.1. In Section 2.2, we discuss the implications of time-aggregation for the AR(1) model. Finally, Section 2.3 introduces the issues of low process coverage from both a continuous-time modeling and a causal inference perspective. 

\subsection{2.1 Dynamic Models}
Let the continuous variable $t$ represent time, and the variable $k$ denote a measurement occasion at a particular point in time. The AR(1) model describes the relationship between a variable at occasion $k$, $Y_{k}$, and the values of that same variable at the previous occasion, $Y_{k - 1}$. It can be expressed as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:AR1}
    Y_{k} = \phi Y_{k - 1} + \epsilon_{k},
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ represents the autoregressive coefficient of the regression of $Y_{k}$ on $Y_{k - 1}$, and $\epsilon_{k}$ is a residual at occasion $k$ that is typically assumed to be mean-zero normally distributed with variance $\sigma_{Y}^{2}$. To investigate relationships between variables, the AR(1) model can be extended with an exogenous time-varying predictor $X$. Given that causes need time to exert an effect, typically a lagged version of $X$ is added as a predictor to the model, resulting in 
\begin{equation}
    Y_{k} = \phi Y_{k - 1} + \beta_{k - 1} X_{k - 1} + \epsilon_{k}. 
\end{equation}
Here, $\beta_{k - 1}$ represents the lag-1 effect from $X$ to $Y$. However, it has been argued that when the time interval is large and the causal effect of $X$ under study is relatively rapid, that lagged effects of $X$ should not be added \parencite{mundlak_aggregation_1961}. Instead, lag-0 effects could be added to the model, resulting in the ARX(1) model. It is given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ARX1}
    Y_{k} = \phi Y_{k - 1} + \beta_{k} X_{k} + \epsilon_{k}, 
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{k}$ now represents the lag-0 (rapid) effect of the time-dependent predictor on the outcome. Similar discussions about modeling instantaneous effects have taken place in the context of panel data analysis \parencite{greenberg_panel_1979, greenberg_equilibrium_1982}, and more recently by \textcite{muthen_can_2024} in the context of cross-lagged panel modeling using structural equation modeling (SEM). 

\subsection{2.2 Time-Aggregation}
There exist various forms of time-aggregation, also referred to as \textit{aggregation schemes}. In this paper, we discuss time-aggregation that concerns taking the mean across a particular period of time, as this is a relatively common aggregation-scheme in psychological measurements. To this end, we introduce some additional notation below. Let $m$ be the number of time periods that we aggregate over, and suppose that levels of anxiety (i.e., the anxiety-process) vary from day to day. Further suppose for simplicity's sake that each month consists of four weeks, and thus 28 days. As mentioned in the introduction, anxiety is measured in the LISS panel data with the question ``This past month... I felt very anxious.'', implying that participants are expected to average their daily anxiety levels over the past $m = 28$ periods (days). To distinguish between measurements of the disaggregated process ($m = 1$) and aggregated measurements ($m > 1$), we add the subscript $(m)$ to variable names, such that $Y_{k_{(m)}}$ represents the outcome $Y$ at measurement $k$ aggregated over $m$ occasions: Disaggregated measurements are then denoted by $Y_{k_{(1)}}$ and 28-day-aggregated measurements by $Y_{k_{(28)}}$. Furthermore, we focus on aggregate measurements that are ``proper'', that is, aggregate measurements are measured back-to-back and do not overlap. For example, with anxiety measured as 28-day-averages, we would only include the aggregates of day 1 to day 28, day 29 to day 56, day 57 to day 84, and so on in our analysis. Hence, intermediate overlapping frames, such as aggregates from day 1 to day 28, day 2 to day 29, day 3 to day 30, are not included. Figure~\ref{fig:notation} illustrates this notation for aggregation over $m = 2, 3$ periods.

For this aggregation scheme, it is interesting to compare the parameter coefficients of dynamic models based on aggregated data to the data generating mechanism (i.e., the model that generated the disaggregated data). To distinguishing between the parameters of the data generating mechanism ($m = 1$) and those from models based on aggregated data ($m > 2$), we therefore concatenate the subscript ${(m)}$ to the parameters of dynamic models. \textcite{amemiya_effect_1972, hickman_effects_1972} study consequences of aggregation for the AR(1) model, comparing the autoregressive parameter $\phi_{(1)}$ in the disaggregated model in Equation~\ref{eq:AR1}, to the autoregressive parameter from the aggregated model given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:AR1-aggregated}
    Y_{k_{(m)}} = \phi_{(m)} Y_{k_{(m)} - 1} + e_{k_{(m)}},
\end{equation} 
with $\phi_{(m)}$ representing the autoregressive parameter of an aggregated process. In earlier research, \textcite{ironmonger_note_1959, nerlove_estimation_1959, telser_discrete_1967} study the dynamics of an aggregated process by comparing the aggregated autoregressive parameter $\phi_{(m)}$ to $\phi_{(1)}^{m}$. However, \textcite{amemiya_effect_1972} explain that $\phi_{(m)}$ and $\phi_{(1)}^{m}$ are generally not equivalent because the aggregation introduces a first-order moving average error structure at the disaggregated level. The problem is that by aggregating data, we lose information at the disaggregated level that is needed to model the first-order moving average structure at the disaggregate level that exists in the data. For this reason, \textcite{amemiya_effect_1972} argue that the dynamics of an aggregated process are qualitatively different dynamics than those from the disaggregated process, even though the aggregated measurements are created from the disaggregated process. 

To better understand the relationship between $\phi_{(m)}$ (for $m > 1$) and $\phi_{(1)}$, \textcite{hickman_effects_1972} express the OLS-estimate of $\phi_{(m)}$ as a function $\phi_{(1)}$. They show that for a covariance stationary process (i.e., when $|\phi_{(1)}| < 1$ and with constant variance and covariance over time), $\phi_{(m)}$ is larger than $\phi_{(1)}^{m}$. \textcite{hickman_effects_1972} also generalize their analysis to more complicated models such as the ARX(1), comparing both the disaggregated autoregressive parameter $\phi_{(1)}$ and time-dependent effect of $X$, $\beta_{(1)}$ with their aggregated counterparts $\phi_{(m)}$ and $\beta_{(m)}$. While they are able to compute the probability limit of the coefficients of the aggregated ARX(1) model as a function of the disaggregated process, they note that ``This relationship is so complicated that no general tendencies can be observed until simplifying approximations are made'' \parencite[][p. 689]{amemiya_effect_1972}, and resort to simulations. We similarly rely on simulations to study the impact of time-aggregation and low process coverage in extended scenarios. 

\subsection{2.3 Process Coverage} 
In addition to time-aggregation of variables, panel data are also commonly characterized by repeated measurements at relatively large intervals. In the past decades, psychological and behavioral researchers have increasingly understood many psychological processes as \textit{continuous time processes}. With panel designs, researchers attempt to systematically ``tap into'' (sample from) such ongoing processes by measuring variables at discrete time points, while the process in between subsequent discrete time points remains unobserved. A critical issue here is the decision of how much time to allow for between measurements occasions, as the parameter estimates from typical discrete-time statistical models are dependent on the chosen time-interval \parencite{gollob_taking_1987}. That is, when using the LISS panel to regress anxiety levels on previous anxiety levels and social media use, the resulting estimates of autoregressive and cross-lagged effects refer to effects that take place over one year; if measurements had instead been taken every six months, analyses would have likely results in different autoregressive and cross-lagged effects even when the underlying causal process remains unchanged. The time interval between measurements in combination with the time-aggregation of those measurements determines the degree to which a process is actually captured (i.e., the process coverage) and how much remains unobserved \parencite{hamaker_within-between_2023}.  

In the context of panel data, measurements are typically taken at monthly or yearly intervals, but it is not unreasonable to assume many psychological processes of interest are actually occurring at a (much) finer time scale \parencite{dorman_optimal_2015, collins_effect_2002}. The continuous time (CT) framework explicitly models the dependency of parameters to time-lags using stochastic differential equations, and therefore allows us to link parameter estimates from measurements at longer intervals, to processes at finer intervals \parencite{voelkle_sem_2012, oud_continuous_2000}. For example, given a disaggregated day-to-day AR(1) process with autoregressive effect $\phi_{(1)}$, it is easy to show that the expected autoregressive relationship when measurements are taken only every two days (i.e., skipping every other day of measurement) is $\phi_{(1)}^{2}$. This logic similarly applies to the processes of aggregated data, where the autoregressive relationship $\phi_{(m)}$ across every second measurement occasion is $\phi_{(m)}^{2}$. By this logic, you would expect the autoregressive parameter of a day-to-day process to approach zero as the time interval between repeated measurements increases to months or years as is typical of panel data (assuming $|\phi_{(1)}| < 1$). For ARX(1) models, the instantaneous effect of time-dependent exposures $\beta_{0_{(m)}}$ is independent of the time-interval between repeated measures, whereas the autoregressive parameter of the ARX(1) model has the same dependency on the time-interval as the the autoregressive parameter in the AR(1) model \parencite{van_montfort_understanding_2018}. For dynamic models with multiple variables---such as the lag-1 vector autoregressive, VAR(1), model---the relationship between parameters at varying time lags is more complex due to interacting direct and indirect effects in the underlying continuous-time process, but is well-understood and described \parencite{oud_sem_2018}. 

While the continuous time framework has been proposed as a useful tool to make inferences about the underlying short-term process with measurements that were taken at a different measurement frequency, the unobserved part of the process in between measurement occasions does present some potential problems from a causal inference perspective. Unbiased estimation of the causal effects assumes that all confounders have been measured such that they can be adjusted for in a statistical analysis. However, suppose that two processes, such as anxiety levels and social media use, vary in discrete time every day, but are observed only every two days. In this situation, the unobserved intermittent levels of anxiety and social media use can act as unobserved confounders that prevent unbiased estimation of particular effects \parencite{zhang_causal_2011}. Figure~\ref{fig:DAG_ARX1_missingFrames} illustrates this with a directed acyclic graph (DAG) for an ARX(1) process. Here, $k = 1$ the first measurement which takes place on day 1, $k = 2$ denotes the second measurement which takes place on day 3, $k = 3$ denotes the third measurement which takes place on day 5, etc. The non-integer $k$-values represent existing, but unobserved levels of anxiety and social media use due to the systematic sampling scheme. From Figure~\ref{fig:DAG_ARX1_missingFrames_DGM}, it can be seen that for lag-0 effects of social media use on anxiety levels at occasion $k$, the social media use at the previous day is a common cause and thus requires adjustment for when attempting to estimate the lag-0 effect $\beta_{0_{(1)}}$. However, when measurements are taken only every second day as in Figure~\ref{fig:DAG_ARX1_missingFrames_observed}, social media use at the previous day remains unobserved. Hence estimates based on observed data $\Tilde{\beta}_{0_{(1)}}$ will be biased for the true lag-0 effect $\beta_{0_{(1)}}$ that generated the data. This concern similarly applies when analyzing aggregated data, and is the focus of Scenario 1 in our simulation study.

\section{3. Methods}
For relatively simple data generating mechanisms we can reason about, and analytically derive how time-aggregation and low process coverage independently impact the parameter estimates from dynamic models. In the current section, we setup a simulation study to assess their \textit{combined} impact on parameter estimation in common analysis models for panel data. The simulations comprise of three different scenarios, each including various degrees of time aggregation and process coverage. Scenario 1 focuses on single subject data with many repeated measures; Scenario 2 extends the simulations of single subject data with a time-varying confounder; Scenario 3 changes the aggregation scheme and systematic sampling scheme to match that of typical panel data, decreasing the number of observed repeated measures and increasing the number of observed units. In line with the running example, we focused on the effect of a presumed binary cause (social media usage) on a continuous outcome (anxiety), and we assume this process to be occurring relatively rapidly compared to the measurement frequency of panel data, for example, within a day. Annotated R code used for the data generation and analyses in this paper can be found in the online supplementary materials at \url{https://jeroendmulder.github.io/time-aggregation}. 

\subsection{3.1 Data Generation}
For all scenarios, we generate data for the binary time-varying exposure $X_{t}$ (0 = no social media use, 1 = social media use) and continuous outcome $Y_{t}$ at time points $t = 1, 2, 3, ..., T$. Within each scenario we inspect multiple experimental conditions, preprocessing the data in different ways to reflect various degrees of time aggregation and process coverage due to different systematic sampling schemes. This ultimately results in the observed $X_{k_{(m)}}$ and $Y_{k_{(m)}}$, which are used in analyses. A burn-in period of a hundred time points was used to remove the influence of initial values. For each condition, a thousand replications were simulated. Data were generated in base R \parencite[version 4.4.1;][]{r_core_team_r_2024} and C++ \parencite{stroustrup_c_2013} using the R packages Rcpp \parencite[version 1.0.13;][]{eddelbuettel_rcpp_2011} and RcppArmadillo \parencite[version 14.0.2-1;][]{eddelbuettel_rcpparmadillo_2014}. 

Scenario 1 is intended to investigate the combined influence of time-aggregation and low process coverage in a relatively simple setting. The data generating mechanism is a bivariate day-to-day process with lag-0 causal effects from $X_{k_{(1)}}$ to $Y_{k_{(1)}}$ as visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:DAG_ARX1_missingFrames_DGM}. The exposure is an exogenous Markov Chain process with a transition probability of 0.119 for switching from state 0 (no social media use) to state 1 (social media use) and a transition probability of 0.5 for switching from state 1 to state 0. This implies an long-term state means of 0.8075 for state 0 and 0.1925 for state 1 \parencite{hoel_introduction_1986}. In Scenarios 2 and 3, the transition probabilities are made dependent on covariates and previous outcome. The outcome is generated as Equation~\ref{eq:ARX1} with $\phi_{(1)} = 0.5$, $\beta_{0_{(1)}} = 0.5$, and the residual normally distributed with variance set to $\sigma_{Y}^{2} = 1$. In this scenario, we aggregate the data over $m = 1, 2, 3$ periods and systematically sample every first, every second, and every third observation. Figure~\ref{fig:scenario1} illustrates this for the case of aggregation over two time periods ($m = 2$) and sampling every second observation. Of interest is how the parameter estimates based on analyses on aggregated data under a systematic sampling scheme ($\Tilde{\phi}_{(m)}$ and $\Tilde{\beta}_{0_{(m)}}$) compare to the parameters in the data generating mechanism. Combined, this scenario comprises of nine experimental conditions. The total number of time points $T$ was adjusted per experimental condition to ensure a thousand observations for each condition.

Scenario 2 extends the simulations for single subject data with a continuous time-varying covariate $L_{t}$ that confounds the relationship between $X_{t}$ and $Y_{t}$. $L_{t}$ is generated by
\begin{equation*}
    L_{t} = \alpha L_{t - 1} + \epsilon_{L,t},
\end{equation*}
with $\alpha_{(1)} = 0.5$ and the residual $e_{L, t}$ normally distributed with variance set to $\sigma_{L}^{2} = 1$. The transition probabilities of the exposure are now made to be dependent on the previous outcome $Y$ and current covariate $L$. Depending on the current state of the exposure $X_{t}$, the next exposure state $X_{t + 1}$ is given by the logistic regression models 
\begin{align*}
    p(X_{t + 1} = 1 | X_{t} = 0) &= expit(\delta_{10, 0} + \delta_{10, 1} Y_{t} + \delta_{10, 2} L_{t + 1}), \\ 
    p(X_{t + 1} = 0 | X_{t} = 1) &= expit(\delta_{01, 0} + \delta_{01, 1} Y_{t} + \delta_{01, 2} L_{t + 1}),
\end{align*}
where $expit(\cdot) = \frac{1}{(1 + exp(-(\cdot))}$.  Parameter values for the transition from state 0 to state 1 were set to $\delta_{10, 0} = -2$, representing a high baseline probability of remaining in state 0, $\delta_{10, 1} = -0.5$, implying a negative effect of anxiety on social media use (i.e., increased anxiety decreases likelihood of subsequent social media use), and $\delta_{10, 2} = 1$, representing a positive effect of the covariate on likelihood of social media use. Parameter values for the transition from state 1 to state 0 were set to $\delta_{01, 0} = 0$, representing an even baseline probability of switching to ``no social media use'', and $\delta_{01, 1} = 1$ and $\delta_{01, 2} = 1$, implying that increased stress and covariate values increase the likelihood of switching to ``no social media use''. The outcome is generated following
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ARX1-covariate}
    Y_{it} = \phi Y_{t - 1} + \beta_{0} X_{it} + \gamma_{0} L_{t} + \epsilon_{Y,t}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma_{0} = 0.8$. $\phi$, $\beta_{0}$, and $\sigma_{Y}^{2}$ were set to the same population values as in Scenario 1. Similarly, we aggregated data generated under this mechanism over $m = 1, 2, 3$ periods and systematically sampled every first, every second, and every third time point. Again, the total number of time points $T$ was adjusted per experimental condition to ensure a thousand observations per condition. Figure~\ref{fig:scenario2} illustrates the data generating mechanism for the case of aggregation over two time periods ($m = 2$) and sampling every second observation.

\subsection{3.2 Estimation}
For Scenarios 1 and 2 we analyzed the data using a linear ARX(1) model. That is, in Scenario 1, we specified the ARX(1) model in Equation~\ref{eq:ARX1}; for Scenario 2, we specified an ARX(1) model while additionally controlling for current time-varying covariate $L_{t}$, see Equation~\ref{eq:ARX1-covariate}. In the disaggregated data, these models provide unbiased estimates for the causal lag-0 effect of $X$ on $Y$. Therefore, any bias in the parameter estimates in other conditions is due to the influence of time aggregation and missing intermittent variables due to the systematic sampling. The models were fitted as Dynamic Structural Equation Models \parencite[with the data in long-format;][]{asparouhov_dynamic_2018} in Mplus \parencite[version 8.11;][]{muthen_mplus_2017}.

In Scenario 3, the number of observed measurements available for analysis is reduced from a thousand to five per subject, and the number of subjects increased to 500. Such panel data necessitate a different modeling strategy. Within psychology and related sciences, a popular approach is analyze the data in wide-format using cross-lagged panel models within the structural equation modeling (SEM) framework \parencite{zyphur_data_2020, zyphur_data_2020-1}. As we are only interested in the effect of social media use on stress, we made use of unidirectional versions of these models. Specifically, we specified a wide-format random intercept ARX(1) model, RI-ARX(1), in the SEM framework while controlling for $L_{k}$ \parencite{muthen_can_2024}. The lag-0 effects from $L_{k}$ and $X_{k}$, and the lag-1 effect of $Y_{k - 1}$ to $Y_{k}$ were constrained to be equal over time. The time-varying exposure $X_{k}$ and covariate $L_{k}$ were allowed to freely covary with each other. Again, there is no model misspecification as all specified relationships are correctly modeled (i.e., the specified relationships are correctly specified as linear and the residual is correctly assumed to come from a normal distribution). Any bias that occurs in the parameter estimates is therefore due to the effect of time aggregation and missing intermittent variables that confound the effect of interest. The RI-ARX(1) is estimated using the R package lavaan \parencite[version 0.6-18][]{rosseel_lavaan_2012}.

\section{5. Discussion}
Time-aggregation of measurements and low process coverage due to systematic sampling over time are two very common temporal features of panel data. A specific time-aggregation or systematic sampling scheme in the design of a panel study can be selected for a diverse set of methodological and practical reasons \parencites{dorman_timing_2014}. For example, aggregation is sometimes used as a method for dealing with measurement error \parencite{rushton_behavioral_1983}, and increasing the interval between repeated measures decreases participant burden while still enabling researchers to measure participants over extended periods of time. However, more generally, substantive literature and theories in the social sciences are notoriously uninformative about the time scale at which to measure a process of interest \parencite{collins_analysis_2006}. For this reason, \textcite{collins_analysis_2006, dorman_optimal_2014} and many methodological scientists have stated that decisions about the time scale of a study design are often ``(...) left to intuition, chance, convenience, or tradition.'' \parencite[][p. 533]{mitchell_building_2001}. Similarly, \textcite{collins_effect_2002} expresses concern about the lack of awareness about the impact of temporal design choices on the results of a study: \begin{quote} ``Scientific, theoretical, or conceptual, as opposed to logistical, reasons for the choice of temporal design are rarely mentioned, and equally rare is any discussion of how this choice might have affected the statistical and substantive conclusions drawn from the study.'' \end{quote}. 

The aim of this paper was therefore to explore and demonstrate the impact of time-aggregation and systematic sampling on a researchers ability to estimate effects of a time-varying exposure in an underlying causal process. While the topic of time-aggregation has received some attention in the econometric literature in dynamic models, it has been severely understudied in the social sciences. For relatively simply autoregressive processes such as the AR(1) and ARX(1) with one time-dependent covariate, there exists some analytical work on time-aggregation and systematic sampling. For example, for the AR(1) and ARX(1) models, it can be derived how the autoregressive effect (and effect of the time-dependent predictor in the ARX1) in aggregated data are a function of the parameters governing the disaggregated process \parencite[cf.][]{amemiya_effect_1972, brewer_consequences_1973}. Furthermore, the continuous-time literature describes how parameters of dynamic models change when the time-interval between repeated measures changes (e.g., due to systematic sampling). However, deriving analytically their combined impact for slightly more complex models is challenging \parencite{amemiya_effect_1972, petkovic_aggregation_2009}. We therefore presented simulation results for three different scenarios, including a process with a time-varying exposure and outcome for $N = 1$, a process with time-varying exposure, outcome, and covariate for $N = 1$, and a process with a time-varying exposure, outcome, and covariate for multiple units but limited repeated measures per unit. Our focus has been on the lag-0 causal effect of $X$ on $Y$. This effect is of interest for situations where the time interval is large but the causal effect under study is relatively rapid, or when there is an implicit time-lag in the measurements of variables \parencite[e.g., the exposure refers to something that happened \textit{the past week} and the outcome relates to something \textit{at the moment of measurement}/``right now'';][]{muthen_can_2024, mundlak_aggregation_1961}. 

Generally, a mismatch between the temporal characteristics of a causal process and the time-scale of the observed data due to time-aggregation or systematic sampling leads to a bias in estimates of $\beta_{0}$. For the systematic sampling, bias in estimates of $\beta_{0}$ occurs because unmeasured intermitted variables act as unobserved time-varying confounders that researchers cannot adjust for. For time-aggregation, the bias occurs because time-aggregation changes the nature of the process itself \parencite{amemiya_effect_1972}. The direction and size of the bias, however, is very much dependent on the underlying causal process: We observed a moderately strong upwards bias for Scenario 1 (i.e., without time-varying covariate) but a strong negative bias for Scenarios 2 and 3. In Scenario 3, the situation that mirrors that of panel data on a daily process, the mismatch of time scales was so sever that it resulted in an average estimated lag-0 effect of $X$ to $Y$ of -0.299 with a coverage rate of 0, while the true population effect was 0.5. 

\subsection{5.1 Implications and Limitations}
This paper demonstrates, in line with previous researchers on this topic, the enormous importance of making time scales a central aspect of of theories. Without specificity about the time frame of ones variables and the speed at which concepts under study change, it is not possible to make an informed decision about how to setup a new study, nor is it possible to evaluate if an existing dataset contains the information needed to answer a research question. This is by no means a new insight, but we do hope that these simulations once again underline the important of this point \parencite[cf.][]{hamaker_within-between_2023, mitchell_building_2001, dorman_temporal_2014, dorman_optimal_2015}.
